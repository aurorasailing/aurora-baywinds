Reading the water

Why the water temperature matters

How warm the water is sounds like a comfort question. It isn't. It sets how deep a loaded ship rides, and for a small boat it decides the one thing that counts after a capsize — the cold. The number on the briefing is read from a satellite; this is why it's worth reading at all.

Warm water floats less

Water isn't always the same weight. Warm water is lighter than cold — less dense — and fresh water, like the river water that flows in at the top of the bay, is lighter than salty water. A ship floats at the depth where it pushes aside its own weight in water. In lighter water it has to sit a little lower to push aside enough. So the very same ship, loaded the same, rides deeper on a warm day than on a cold one.

Same ship, same load: the water's density sets how deep it floats — and how much room is left under the keel.

The gap under the keel

That gap has a name — under-keel clearance, the space between the bottom of the ship and the seabed. The big ships that come up the bay's dredged channels, the ones the briefing tells you to keep well clear of, run with very little water beneath them. Sometimes less than a metre.

To send each one through safely, the port has to know exactly how deep it is sitting right now — and, as we've just seen, that depends on the water's temperature and saltiness. Read the water wrong and a loaded ship touches the bottom.

What it means for a small boat

What matters to a loaded ship matters to a small boat too: how cold the water is.

Port Phillip is cold-temperate water — roughly 10 °C in winter to under 20 °C in late summer. Cold water, not cold air, is the real danger after a capsize, and it decides whether you want a wetsuit today — cold enough to matter even on a warm day, because it's the water, not the air, that sets the risk if you end up in it.

We don't read the port's figure — it sits inside its own shipping system, if it's held as a temperature at all. So the number on this briefing comes from a satellite instead. Where this comes from

The bay's water temperature on this page is read from satellite — NOAA Coral Reef Watch's CoralTemp (see the how-we-read-it page). The under-keel-clearance work of the bay's big ships is run by OMC International for Ports Victoria. The cold-temperate range for Victorian onshore waters was first documented from continuous monitoring at Point Lonsdale in the 1960s.
