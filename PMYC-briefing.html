Weather

Temperature, wind, tide and what they mean for the crew.

Read the wind, the tide and the change before you launch. Match clothing, sun cover and wetsuits to the day.

Cold and heat: the crew check each other, not only the instructor — the one affected is the last to notice.

  • Cold: shivering that won't stop → off the water and warm up. Shivering stopping while still cold is worse, not better.
  • Heat: confusion, stumbling, or skin that's stopped sweating → Triple Zero (000) and cool fast.
  • In doubt either way, head in.

Full signs and steps on the Cold & Heat page.

Water quality: stormwater after rain lifts the bacteria count — hold off immersion a day or two after heavy rain, worse near drains and the pier outfalls. In season, check the EPA Beach Report for Port Melbourne.

Location

The area, the piers, the sandbank, the traffic.

The area & the launch

  • The triangle: Station Pier — Lagoon Pier — the G marker. Depth about 2–5 m.
  • Launch from the east beach by the club (the Dog Beach) — the shared 5-knot zone, and no access lane, so 5 knots the whole way to 200 m. Stay clear of the Port Melbourne lifesaving-club swim-only zone.
  • Sandbank 20–30 m out: seen at high tide, barely covered at low. Wade the boat onto / past it to launch; head into the wind just short of it to land. Don't copy the cat sailors up the sand.

The piers

  • Lagoon Pier (ESE): wide berth. The stormwater drain ~100 m short is the turn-back line. Fishing lines; kids jumping.
  • Station Pier (W): keep 50 m and well clear of a berthed ship.

Traffic, marks & wildlife

  • Jet skis are the number-one hazard, even in the 5-knot band. Swimmers, SUPs, dogs on the Dog Beach, anchored fishing boats between club and G marker, water police.
  • The G marker is a race mark for the big clubs — leave it when a yacht fleet is heading for it.
  • Dolphins on flat northerly mornings; jellyfish in season.

Communication

Signals, and the one that overrides everything.

  • Help — one fist overhead, or two fists overhead.
  • Attention, come closer — a low one-hand wave.
  • Fun — any other wave, with a happy face.

Return To Shore (RTS)

From the power boat: whistle, shout, November flag. Every boat returns on its own — shortest and safest way the captain judges. Don't wait for a further signal. Any beach will do, through a swim zone or beyond Lagoon Pier if needed; ideally the club launch. Regroup on the beach.

Radio: VHF generally CH 73.

Safety

Buddy system, capsize, first aid, head knocks.

Buddy system & counting

No one alone, ashore or afloat. The fleet looks after itself and stays together. X3s: small, orange stripe, 1–4 of them. Quest: bigger, white mast float, green accents, only one. Count before, count often, spot the gap — everyone's job.

Capsize drill

Reviewed on land each time. The three that stay with you:

  • Stay calm.
  • Is everyone OK?
  • Hold on to the boat.

Then: bow to wind, weight on the centreboard, take control, collect crew, set lines, sail on. Several boats over at once — stay calm, stay out of the water if you can, wait; someone will come.

First aid

Notify the safety boat first — it radios ahead. Kits: clubhouse upstairs by the tower stairs, and the boat shed by the sailing-school shed. Log what's used; incident report usually needed. Small cut, help yourself; anything real, get assistance.

Head knocks — concussion

A knock to the head doesn't always show at once, and the one hit is the worst judge of it — so the crew watches.

  • Knocked out, even for a second → Triple Zero (000); treat the neck as injured too.
  • A hard knock → come in and sit it out; don't sail on to test it.
  • A light bump → keep an eye out; it can come on over hours.

If in doubt, stay ashore or head back in — rest, and keep watching. The graded response and signs are on the Concussion page.
